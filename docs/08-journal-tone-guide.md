# Journal Tone Guide

This document is the single source of truth for writing wolf expedition journal lines in Working Wolves. All journal entries — across mob encounters, travel, ore/wood discovery, hazards, rare events, and expedition lifecycle — must conform to this guide.

## What the journal is

The journal is the wolf's first-person log of a completed expedition, read by the player at the dog bed. The loot is already in the inventory; the journal is the story of how it got there. Players read it at leisure to understand what their wolf went through.

The voice writing these entries is the wolf itself — a real Minecraft wolf (domesticated, working, loyal) reporting back to its owner. It is competent at its job. It notices the world through its senses first: smell, then sound, then sight. It is interested in things without being dramatic about them.

All wolves share this baseline voice. Individual entries can vary in humor or curiosity level, but the fixed elements — sensory-first observation, legible outcomes, no philosophical abstraction — never change.

---

## The primary register: Sensory Witness

The default voice for all journal lines. The wolf reports what its senses caught, in the order they caught them, and what happened as a result.

**Why it works:** It is unmistakably a dog. It grounds the entry in observable fact. The sensory anchor does the flavor work without obscuring the outcome.

```
"Heard the bow creak before I saw it. Ducked low. Arrow in the dirt."
"Wind was wrong — didn't smell it until it was already hissing. Got clear. Mostly."
"Smelled the iron before I found it. Damp stone, that sharp rust underneath."
"The click before the hiss. Backed up."
"Pine smoke from the east. Old fire, not ours."
```

The wolf has a nose that reaches farther than its eyes. Lean on this. A wolf encountering a skeleton archer hears the bowstring first. A wolf finding ore smells the mineral before it sees the glint. A wolf sensing danger detects it through the air before it processes it visually.

**Sensory opener vs. pure atmosphere:** A sensory opener is an action — the wolf used a sense and got information. "Heard the bow creak" is a sensory fact; it tells you what the wolf did (listened) and what it learned (danger). This is not atmosphere. Pure atmosphere is setting description with no wolf action embedded: "The shadows pooled in the corners of the cave." The first is always allowed in combat lines. The second is not.

---

## Secondary register: Curious Observer

Used for zone 2 (deep/dangerous biome) travel lines, rare events, and any encounter where the wolf pauses to notice something genuinely strange or interesting. The wolf is paying attention to the world — not wonder-struck, not performatively cool, just present.

```
"Redstone pulses when you tap it. Tested this several times."
"Copper turns green down here. Don't know why. Brought green copper."
"Doesn't have eyes, exactly. Still found me. Strange animal."
"Spider nest — hundreds of them. Smelled it before I saw it. Left quickly. Professionally."
"Lapis. Blue like the sky, but underground. Weird place for it."
```

The curiosity is dog-specific: the wolf stops to sniff something, notices a thing that doesn't fit the pattern, files it away. It's not philosophical reflection — it's alert attention.

**Watch for:** "interesting" and "strange" can become verbal tics. Use them deliberately, not as filler.

---

## Lifecycle register: Competent and Tired

Used for departure, arrival, success, failure, recall, and death lines. These categories carry emotional undertone as their primary payload — the wolf is not reporting an event, it's marking a state. The voice here is matter-of-fact and a little worn.

```
"Again."                                                          (departure)
"The bed smelled like sleep. Left it."                            (departure)
"Sometimes that's what the job looks like."                       (failure)
"Third trip this week. The tunnel knows me now."                  (success)
"Stepped onto the bed. Smelled like home. Was home."              (arrival)
"Bag down. Paws up. Done."                                        (arrival)
```

Arrival lines get one sensory anchor — smell of the bed, the weight off the paws, warmth — before closing. They don't just confirm return; they register it in the wolf's body.

This register works in small, concentrated doses. The humor (when it lands) comes from understatement of real effort, not from performing stoicism.

## The three rules

### 1. Fact before flavor — except for resource discovery

**Combat and hazard lines:** Must deliver a legible outcome in the first sentence. Sensory openers are allowed (they are facts in themselves), but they must be followed immediately by an action or outcome. No line ends without the reader knowing what happened.

**Resource discovery lines (ore, wood):** These follow a looser version of this rule. The wolf can open with two sensory sentences before the outcome, because finding ore is not a survival-stakes moment. The sensory approach is the pleasure of the find.

```
"Smells like nothing. Shines like everything. Took the whole vein."   ← ore, sensory → sensory → fact: allowed
"Heard the bow creak. Ducked. Arrow in the dirt."                     ← combat, sensory → action → result: required
```

### 2. Legible outcome verb in combat and hazard lines

Every line describing a fight or hazard must contain a verb that clearly resolves the encounter.

Good outcome verbs: *backed up, got clear, felled it, dealt with it, turned around, moved on, climbed out, cleared it, came home, lost ground, got bitten, took the hit.*

**Do:** "Heard the bow creak. Ducked. Arrow in the dirt."  
**Don't:** "The arrow passed where my shoulder had been. Where my shoulder no longer was." — survival is implied, not stated; no action verb.

### 3. Atmosphere belongs in travel and lifecycle

Travel and lifecycle lines carry no unresolved stakes — the wolf is moving, not in danger. Pure atmosphere is allowed and encouraged there. Combat and hazard lines must have a sensory fact or an outcome verb; setting description alone is not enough.

**Do (travel):** "The forest grew quiet." — fine; nothing is at stake yet.  
**Do (combat):** "Creeper behind a tree. Backed up fast." — fact, then action.  
**Don't (combat):** "A thing built around a single regret, looking for someone to share it with." — no outcome, no wolf action, pure interpretation.

## What to avoid

**The two-sentence pivot.** `[poetic observation] + [outcome deflection]`. "The work asked a lot. Gave more." / "Took the worst of it. Still here." The second clause exists only to confirm survival. Once the pattern is visible, every entry reads as a performance. Real terseness does not remind you it's being terse.

**Philosophical attribution.** The wolf does not know what a creeper "regrets." It does not know what a zombie "used to be proud of." It knows what things smell and sound like, and what it did about them. Stay in the wolf's actual epistemic world.

**Abstract nouns as punchlines.** "A hunger that did not understand it was no longer hungry." / "A wound that wanted to stay open." These are word-workshop lines, not wolf reports. The wolf does not traffic in abstractions.

*Note: Rules 1–2 above are the structural version of this prohibition. "What to avoid" explains the failure mode; the three rules give the testable check.*

---

## Length

**One to three short sentences.** The player scans the log — they don't read it top to bottom every time. Lines that front-load atmosphere and bury the fact fail because they depend on a reading mode the player is not in.

First sentence: the fact (what happened, what was found, what the wolf did).  
Second sentence: the voice (how the wolf experienced it, what it noticed, a sensory detail).  
Third sentence: optional coda, used sparingly.

## Category-specific notes

**Mob encounters:** Name the mob or describe it recognizably (by sound/smell/behavior first). Resolve the encounter. Add sensory or curious detail only after resolution.

**Ore/wood discovery:** Anchor in the physical sensation of finding it — smell, the sound of the pickaxe hitting the right stone, the way light catches. Don't abstract into "it was valuable." The wolf doesn't trade; it just found a thing that glitters. Two sensory sentences before the fact are allowed here (see Rule 1).

**Hazards:** Scale the stakes to the severity.
- *Light:* Minor setback, physical inconvenience. The wolf is fine but something went slightly wrong.
- *Moderate:* Injury or real effort. The wolf got hurt, or had to work hard to get out. Name the injury in physical terms — a bite, a cut, a bruise, heat on the fur — not in abstract terms ("a wound that wanted to stay open").
- *Severe:* Near-death. Survival must be unambiguous; the reader should feel the closeness. State or strongly imply the physical damage. "Came home with proof" is too vague — proof of what? Name it: limping, singed fur, blood, a missing chunk of the ear.

**Rare events:** These are the journal's highlight moments. Lean into Curious Observer — the wolf encountered something unusual and is reporting it with appropriate attention. Still resolve clearly.

**Departure:** Short. Sensory grounding in the bed (warmth, smell) followed by departure. One or two sentences.

**Arrival:** Short. One sensory anchor (smell of the bed, weight off paws, warmth) before or alongside the confirmation of return. Not just a logistics confirmation.

**Death lines:** Written as if the journal just stops. Read by the player after the wolf is gone. Spare. Final. No lingering imagery.

## Writing for the log stream

Any individual line appears in a long sequential stream of other lines. A player reading back a full expedition sees travel notes, encounters, discovery entries, hazard reports, and lifecycle lines all mixed together. Design for this context, not for the line in isolation.

**Pool-level structural variety.** Within any single `journal_lines` array, no two entries should open with the same word, and no more than two should follow the same sentence structure. Structural repetition at the pool level is invisible when reviewing one file but glaring when five consecutive log entries follow the same pattern.

**Lead with the most important noun or verb.** Players scan, not read. "§lSkeleton.§l Heard it before I saw it." lands faster than "Heard something rattling in the dark. Turned out to be a skeleton." Front-loading the key noun lets the eye skip to it.

**Lifecycle lines are rhythm markers.** Departure, arrival, success, and failure lines break up combat and discovery clusters. Keep them shorter than event lines — their structural role in the log is as chapter dividers, not narration.

**Mix registers across a pool.** A travel pool of all-Sensory-Witness entries reads flat over a long expedition even if each line is good. Each category's pool should include a spread across the three registers — some sensory-led, some Curious Observer, some brief and tired — so the log has natural rhythm.

## Rich text

Journal lines support Minecraft §-format codes for inline bold, italic, and color. Used well, formatting breaks visual pattern repetition across a long log and helps the player scan for what matters without reading every word. Used badly, it decorates without informing.

### Color prefixes (semantic, per line)

Embed the category color at the start of each line. This replaces any code-side keyword detection — the color is explicit and author-controlled.

| Category | Code | Rendered color | Use for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Discovery | `§a` | Bright green | Ore found, wood found, rare event reward |
| Hazard | `§c` | Red | Combat result with injury, moderate/severe hazard |
| Neutral | *(none)* | Default gray | Travel, lifecycle, light hazard, low-stakes encounters |

After a `§a` or `§c` prefix, the whole line renders in that color unless overridden. `§r` mid-line resets to Minecraft's white — **avoid `§r` mid-line on colored lines**; place it only at the very end (or omit it entirely and let the color run to end of line).

```
"§aFound iron. Smelled it before I saw it."
"§cTook a bite getting clear. Made it."
"Followed a scent through the trees."
```

### Inline emphasis

**Bold `§l`** — use on the key noun in a line: the mob name, the resource name, the critical event. Helps the eye jump to the most important word when scanning a long log. Leave bold on for the rest of the line rather than using `§r` mid-line on already-colored entries.

```
"§a§lDiamond§l vein, six deep. Took all of it."
"§c§lCave-in.§l The section leaned. Left quickly."
"§lSkeleton§l on the ridge. Heard the bow creak first."
```

**Italic `§o`** — use on sensory detail sentences: what the wolf smelled, heard, or felt. Creates a visual register shift that marks the Sensory Witness voice. Works best as the second sentence in a line, after the fact is already delivered.

```
"Got clear. §oBurning-hair smell on the wind for an hour after.§o"
"§aFound copper. §oThat green-rust smell, faint, then stronger.§o"
```

### Formatting on lifecycle lines

Do not add bold or italic to departure, arrival, success, failure, or recall lines. Those lines work through compression and emotional tone. Formatting undercuts the register — a bolded word implies there's something to scan for, which is the wrong expectation for a two-word arrival line.

### What not to use

- **Underline `§n`** — looks like a hyperlink. False affordance in a read-only context.
- **Strikethrough `§m`** — no semantic meaning in a journal.
- **Obfuscated `§k`** — genuinely unreadable. No use case.
- **Formatting on every line** — the scan value of bold/italic comes from scarcity. If every line has a bolded noun, nothing stands out. Reserve it for the one word in the line the player most needs to notice.
